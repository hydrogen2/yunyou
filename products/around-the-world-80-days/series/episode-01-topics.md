## Block A — the question (topics 1–5, ~8½ min)

### T1 · How long would it take you?
`Fact` · **90 s** · opens cold, no title card

**Hook (first 6 seconds, works muted):** *"How long would it take you to go all the way round the world?"*

**What I say:**
> Right now, today, the answer is about forty-four hours. In November 2024 three men from Hong Kong
> went the whole way round on ordinary scheduled flights — tickets anyone could buy — in 44 hours and 33 minutes.
> Higher up it's faster. The space station laps the planet every ninety minutes; the people on board
> watch sixteen sunrises a day.
> Now take all of that away. No aircraft. No radio. In 1872 the honest answer to the question was:
> nobody actually knew.
> So somebody turned it into a bet. Eighty days, half a fortune — and for the next hundred and fifty
> years, real people kept trying to beat him.

**Why it stays:** it makes the viewer answer a question before we ask for any attention, and the
ninety-minute figure is the kind of thing people repeat. It also states the show's subject in 15 seconds —
the thing Day 1's cut never did.

**Facts:** 44 h 33 min 39 s, Cham-Kai Yip / Ricky Li / Hanson Wen, 21–23 Nov 2024 **[new, Guinness — high]** ·
ISS orbital period ~90 min **[well established, verify exact figure]**

### T2 · The people who actually raced it
`Story` · **140 s** · the topic you asked for

**Hook:** *"Which is strange — because he never existed."*

**What I say:**
> The book came out, and people immediately started treating a novel as a challenge.
> An American called George Francis Train insisted he'd already done it — eighty days, in 1870, two years
> before the book — and spent the rest of his life saying so. His line was: *"Verne stole my thunder. I'm Phileas Fogg."*
> Then in November 1889 a New York newspaper reporter named Nellie Bly was given two days' notice, one bag,
> and a ticket. She went east. On the way she stopped at Amiens, in France, and met Jules Verne himself.
> She came home in **72 days, 6 hours, 11 minutes and 14 seconds** — and beat a man who did not exist.
> What she didn't know when she left is that a rival magazine had put a second woman, Elizabeth Bisland,
> on a train the same day, going the opposite way round. Bisland lost by four days. She also beat Fogg.
> It never really stopped. In 1928 a Danish newspaper ran a competition in Verne's honour and sent the winner —
> a fifteen-year-old boy called Palle Huld — round the world with aircraft banned. He did it in 44 days.
> Twenty thousand people met him at the station in Copenhagen. He is said to have given Hergé the idea for Tintin.
> Michael Palin did it for the BBC in 1988 without flying: 79 days and 7 hours.
> And there is a sailing trophy named after Verne. The first time anyone won it, in 1993, the time was
> 79 days, 6 hours — under eighty by about seventeen hours. This year it stands at **40 days, 10 hours and 45 minutes.**

**Why it stays:** every one of these is a "tell someone tomorrow" fact, and together they do something no
single fact can — they show that the number 80 became a real measuring stick for 150 years. Bly meeting Verne
on the way past is the hinge of the whole episode.

**Facts [all new, need Fact-Check pass]:** Train quote **[med — widely repeated, want a primary]** ·
Bly 72 d 6 h 11 m 14 s, dep. 14 Nov 1889 09:40 on *Augusta Victoria*, ret. 25 Jan 1890, 24,898 miles,
met Verne at Amiens **[high, but the seconds figure is secondary-source]** · Bisland, *The Cosmopolitan*,
westbound, 76½ days, arrived 30 Jan 1890 **[high]** · Huld 1928, *Politiken* competition, 44 days, no aviation,
crowd of 20,000; Tintin link **[the trip is high; the Tintin claim is "reportedly" — say "is said to have"]** ·
Palin 1988, 79 d 7 h **[high]** · Jules Verne Trophy: Bruno Peyron 1993 79 d 6 h 15 m 56 s → Thomas Coville,
*Sodebo Ultim 3*, 40 d 10 h 45 m 30 s, 2026 **[high]**

**Needs:** portraits of Bly and Bisland (both public domain, easy) · Huld newsreel or press photo · a clean
record-board graphic that builds line by line — this is the episode's second designed asset after the map.

### T3 · The man they were all chasing
`Story` · **95 s**

**Hook:** *"So who were they racing? A man who spent the whole journey trying not to look at it."*

**What I say:**
> London, Wednesday the 2nd of October, 1872. In a club on Pall Mall, Phileas Fogg bets five other members
> twenty thousand pounds — half of everything he has, about two million in today's money — that he can go
> round the Earth and be back in that room in eighty days.
> Here's the part that matters: he isn't betting on himself. He's a man with no visible talents and no
> apparent interests. He's betting on the planet — on whether the railways and the steamers and the canals
> finished in the last three years have actually joined up into one working machine.
> His friends think the world is still too big. He thinks it just closed.
> And in 1872 that was a genuinely open question. That's why it's a bet and not a boast.

**Why it stays:** the reframe — *he's betting on the planet, not on himself* — is the thesis of the whole
series in one sentence, and it makes a bloodless character into an interesting proposition.

**Facts:** F-11 (the wager, £20,000 at Baring's, 21 Dec, 8:45 pm, 115,200 minutes) · F-08 (the five members) ·
£20,000 → ~£2 m **[needs a stated inflation basis — Bank of England calculator; cite it]**

### T4 · Why eighty — the year the world closed
`Map` · **140 s** · THE asset we still owe (G-13)

**Hook:** *"Three years earlier the bet would have been impossible. Not hard. Impossible."*

**What I say:**
> May 1869: two railway companies meet in Utah and America has a line from coast to coast.
> November 1869: the Suez Canal opens, and ships stop going round Africa.
> 1870: India's railways join up across the subcontinent.
> September 1871: a tunnel opens under the Alps.
> And on the 22nd of August 1872 — six weeks before Fogg walks out of his front door — the last stretch of
> telegraph wire is strung across Australia, and for the first time a message can be sent the whole way round
> the world and back.
> Thirty-six thousand poles. Eleven repeater stations. The last inhabited continent joins the network.
> The circuit closes — and then he sets off.
>
> That circuit is the route of this series. Eight episodes, one lap: the Channel and the Alps, the canal
> at Suez, the railway across India with a piece missing, the ports of the East, the Pacific, the line
> across America. The same order, the same places, a hundred and fifty years later.
> And all the way round, the question Fogg is actually betting on. Had the world really closed?
> We're going to find out where it held — and where it very nearly didn't.

**Why it stays:** your own example of what passes the test. It is the series' argument, and it is a fact about
the world rather than about a character.

**It also carries the series intro.** The map has just drawn the circuit; that is the one moment in the
episode where "here is what this series is" costs nothing, because the route is already on screen.
A separate intro card would be a throat-clear, which D9 bans in an opening.

**Treatment note:** large type, one line drawn at a time, four labels maximum on screen at once, dates only.
The Day 1 map failed because it was a print master shown at 0.596 scale — this is drawn for 1080p from scratch.

**Facts:** F-141 (Overland Telegraph, 22 Aug 1872, 36,000 poles, 11 stations) · F-10 · Day 2 F-69 (Fréjus,
17 Sept 1871) · Suez 17 Nov 1869 · Promontory 10 May 1869

### T5 · The tour that really left that month  — ❌ CUT 2026-09-09 (founder)
`Fact` · **70 s**

**Hook:** *"While Verne was inventing this, a travel agent was actually doing it."*

**What I say:**
> In late September 1872 — days before Fogg's fictional departure — Thomas Cook led eight paying customers
> out of Liverpool on the first organised round-the-world tour ever sold. Atlantic steamer, stagecoach across
> America, paddle steamer to Japan, then overland through China and India.
> It cost two hundred guineas. It took two hundred and twenty-two days.
> So the real answer, from the man whose actual job this was, in the actual month: about seven months.
> Fogg's eighty days was not a schedule. It was a provocation.

**Why it stays:** it's the reality check that makes the bet look reckless again, and it lands the difference
between *possible* and *comfortable*. Also a lovely coincidence of timing.

**Facts [new]:** Cook's first world tour, 8 travellers, 200 guineas, 222 days **[med-high]** ·
⚠ **departure date conflicts**: the novel's Wikipedia page says 20 Sept 1872, another source says
26 Sept from Liverpool. Say "late September" until Research settles it.

### T6 · The largest city there had ever been
`Explainer` · **95 s**

**Hook:** *"Fogg is setting out from the biggest city that had ever existed on Earth."*

**What I say:**
> London, 1871 census: 3.9 million people. A quarter bigger than Beijing. Two-thirds bigger than Paris.
> Five times New York. It had been the largest city in the world since about 1825 and it still was.
> The world's largest port, its tonnage up tenfold in eighty years. Twelve main railway termini — no other
> city on earth had anything close. And an underground railway, the first one anywhere, nine years old,
> already carrying nine and a half million people a year.
> Here's the thing people get wrong about Fogg's London. It wasn't old. The Embankment was two years old.
> His station was eight. The clock tower was thirteen.
> He set out from a brand-new city to find out whether the rest of the world had caught up.

**Facts:** F-138, F-139, F-140, F-142

### T7 · Half a mile
`Walk` · **65 s** · **NEW — the anchor. Everything after this hangs off it.**

**Hook:** *"Everything left in this episode happens inside half a mile."*

**What I say:**
> Verne gives Fogg three addresses, and they are all in the same small corner of London.
> A house on Savile Row. A club on Pall Mall. A railway station at Charing Cross.
> He walks the first leg himself, and because he is the man he is, he counts it: five hundred and
> seventy-five paces with the right foot, five hundred and seventy-six with the left.
> Eleven hundred and fifty-one steps. About nine hundred metres.
> That is the whole of London he uses. A man about to go round the Earth spends his last day on it
> inside half a mile.
> So we're going to walk that half mile — because all three of those places turn out to be more
> interesting than the man who used them.

**Why it stays:** it does three jobs in a minute. It plants the geography the next five topics depend on;
it restores the 1,151 steps as *structure* rather than as characterisation, which is what made them dry
before; and the last line tells the viewer the rule of this show, so nothing after it feels like a detour.

**Facts:** F-05 (1,151 steps, ~0.9 km) · F-01 (No. 7 Savile Row) · F-12 (Charing Cross, 8:45)

### T8 · What a London club actually was
`Explainer` · **105 s**

**Hook:** *"Fogg makes the bet in a club. It is worth knowing what that word actually meant."*

**What I say:**
> Not a bar. A private house the size of a palace, where a few hundred men who agreed with each other
> could eat, read, write letters, play cards, and in many of them sleep — all of it staffed with servants,
> none of it for sale.
> You could not buy your way in. You were proposed by a member, seconded by another, then voted on:
> every member dropped a ball into a box, white to admit, black to reject. Enough black balls and you
> were out. That is where the word blackballed comes from.
> At the height of it London had more than four hundred of these places, most of them inside a few hundred
> metres of St James's and Pall Mall. Half of the principal ones had been founded in the previous thirty years.
> This is the second leg of Fogg's walk, and almost every building on it is still standing. Frontage for
> frontage, what he passes is close to what you would see today.

**Facts:** F-123, F-124, F-125, F-126, F-129

### T9 · The Reform — a party built as a palace
`Explainer` · **85 s**

**Hook:** *"The room where Fogg makes his bet is, in real life, a political weapon."*

**What I say:**
> The Reform Club exists for one reason. After the Reform Act of 1832 the Conservatives founded the
> Carlton Club, a few doors along Pall Mall. The other side wanted something "to counter the machinations
> of the Carlton" — their words — and in 1836 they got it.
> So the room where a fictional Englishman bets twenty thousand pounds on the size of the world is,
> in fact, the headquarters of a political faction, built to out-build the opposition down the street.
> It is still there, still a club. It stopped being party-political after the war. In 1981 it became one of
> the first of the old clubs to admit women on equal terms. Its library, given book by book by its members,
> now runs to more than eighty-five thousand volumes.
> Verne, incidentally, never saw the inside of it.

**Facts:** F-127, F-126, F-128 · ⚠ the closing line needs checking — Research to confirm whether Verne
ever visited London clubs. **Cut it if unverified.**

### T10 · Savile Row — 280 metres, and one word
`Walk` · **120 s**

**Hook:** *"Verne gives Fogg a house here — number 7. Here is what he doesn't tell you about the street."*

**What I say:**
> Two hundred and eighty metres, one block behind Regent Street. Built in the 1730s as ordinary gentry
> housing; the tailors arrived around 1803, following the army officers and politicians who lived here.
> Which is why the Savile Row shape — structured shoulder, high armhole — comes from military uniform,
> not from fashion. The two firms at No. 1 started out making army caps and naval coats.
> The street will tell you the word bespoke was born here: cloth "spoken for" by a customer. It wasn't.
> The word is from 1755 — fifty years before the first tailor arrived.
> And in 2008 they lost the word for real. The advertising regulator ruled that a £495 suit, cut and largely
> machine-sewn in Germany, could still be called bespoke. Nobody owns the term.
> So the Row stopped defending the dictionary and started defending geography. Its own standard now demands
> a paper pattern cut for one person, at least fifty hours of hand work, three fittings — and a workshop
> within a hundred yards of this street.
> One last thing about Fogg living here. He leaves for a three-month journey with a single bag: two shirts
> and three pairs of stockings. He lived on the street where one suit takes fifty hours by hand and three
> months to finish — and he packed as though clothes did not exist.

**Why it stays:** this material ate five minutes of the last cut. It is genuinely good and it is worth
exactly one topic. The closing turn is the anchor: it makes the street about *him* in one sentence,
without a word of plot.

**Facts:** F-110, F-111, F-112, F-114, F-115, F-121, F-116 · F-12 (the carpet-bag)

### T11 · No. 1 — where they decided what was still blank
`Story` · **90 s**

**Hook:** *"At the top of Fogg's street is the room where Britain worked out which parts of the world were still unmapped."*

**What I say:**
> From 1870 the Royal Geographical Society had its headquarters at No. 1 Savile Row — where expeditions to
> Africa, Asia and the South Pole were planned, funded and argued about.
> In April 1874 David Livingstone's body lay in that building before it was carried to Westminster Abbey.
> Six doors down the same street, Verne had just put a man who bet he could go round the world without
> looking at any of it.
> One end of a three-minute street: the people who wanted to see everything. The other end: a man who
> crosses the entire planet and notices nothing.
> The Society sold the building to a tailor in 1912 for thirty-eight thousand pounds — partly because that
> firm had dressed so many of the explorers.

**Facts:** F-113

### T12 · Charing Cross — the point London is measured from
`Fact` · **85 s**

**Hook:** *"He begins a journey round the world from the exact spot Britain measures every distance from."*

**What I say:**
> The third address. A bronze horseman at Charing Cross, cast in 1633. Since the early 1800s it has been
> the point every road distance to London is counted from. When a sign in Scotland says LONDON 400,
> it means this statue — not the city, this statue.
> There's a stone cross in the station forecourt that looks medieval. It's Victorian — 1865 — and it's in
> the wrong place. The real one stood where the horseman is now.
> It was the last of twelve. When Eleanor of Castile died in 1290, her body was carried a hundred and eighty
> miles to Westminster over twelve days, and Edward I built a stone cross at every place it rested overnight.
> Charing was the final night, and its cross cost seven times any of the others.
> Parliament ordered it pulled down in 1643. It came down in 1647, after standing 353 years, and the parish
> sold the stone — forty-seven cartloads of it — for paving.
> Fogg's train leaves from a station built on that spot. Every distance in Britain is measured from here.
> At a quarter to nine, a man walks onto a platform and starts measuring something rather larger.

**Facts:** F-135, F-134, F-131, F-132, F-133, F-136 · ⚠ F-130: do **not** repeat the *chère reine* folk etymology

### T13 · A quarter to nine
`Scene` · **80 s** · closes the episode, hands to E2

**Hook:** *"He gave himself ten minutes to pack for the planet."*

**What I say:**
> Fogg leaves the club at twenty-five past seven having won twenty guineas at cards, tells his new servant
> they are going round the world and will start in ten minutes, and packs one carpet-bag: two shirts and
> three pairs of stockings each, a mackintosh, a travelling cloak, and a railway timetable.
> The train for Dover leaves at a quarter to nine.
> It's raining. Passepartout's watch is four minutes slow and set to London, and he has no intention of
> changing it for anybody.
> And somewhere behind them, in an empty house on Savile Row, a gas lamp is still burning that nobody
> turned off — which is going to be funny for about seventy-nine more days.
>
> Ahead of him: a boat across the Channel. A hole under the Alps that opened thirteen months ago.
> A canal three years old. A railway across India with a piece missing. An ocean that takes twenty-two days.
> And somewhere out on the Pacific, something quietly happens to the calendar that nobody on board notices.
> Eighty days. It's raining. He's already gone.

**Facts:** F-12, F-13, F-02 (the watch, four minutes slow)

**The lookout is the series hook, and it must not spoil.** The Pacific line teases the ending without
naming it — "something happens to the calendar" is true, intriguing, and gives nothing away. Never say
date line, never say he gains a day. That is E8's whole payoff and it is the best one we have.

## Where the hooks go — and where they deliberately don't

A cliffhanger every ninety seconds fights the house tone ("unhurried, silence is content"), so hooks
are placed at the four seams that actually carry weight, not between every topic:

| seam | device |
|---|---|
| **T1 → T2** | T1 ends *"real people kept trying to beat him"*; T2 opens *"Which is strange — because he never existed."* A promise, then the turn. |
| **T4, tail** | The series intro, landing on the map that has just drawn the route. Eight episodes, one lap, and the question that runs the whole way round. |
| ~~T5 → T6~~ | Removed with Cook. T4's series promise now hands straight to London: "…where it very nearly didn't." → "Fogg is setting out from the biggest city that had ever existed." |
| **T13, tail** | The lookout: what is ahead of him, ending on the Pacific tease. Hands to E2 and to the series. |

Everywhere else the topics simply follow one another. If the edit feels flat in the middle, the fix is
a better topic, not a manufactured cliffhanger.

## Threads planted here, paid off later
- **Passepartout's watch** is four minutes slow and set to London. He will refuse to change it across the
  entire planet. It is the mechanism of the ending in Episode 8 — plant it in T12 with one clause.
- **The gas** — T12. Pays in E8.
- **Fix and the £55,000 bank robbery** — not in this episode. Verne introduces it here, but it fails the
  interestingness test cold; it lands properly in E3 when the telegram starts chasing him.
- **"Had the world really closed?"** — asked in T4, answered by the series, settled in E8.
