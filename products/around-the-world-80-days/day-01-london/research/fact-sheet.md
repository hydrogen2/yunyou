## B. The real places

| id | fact | confidence | sources | notes |
|----|------|-----------|---------|-------|
| F-14 | The Reform Club is a real, still-operating private members' club at **104 Pall Mall, London SW1Y 5EW**, founded 1836 by Radicals and Whigs who had backed the Great Reform Act of 1832; it later served as the political headquarters of the Liberal Party and is now politically neutral. About 2,700 members; women admitted on equal terms since 1981. | high | [2][3][4] | Founder's brief link confirmed. |
| F-15 | The clubhouse was designed by Charles Barry (chosen 25 Nov 1837 from seven invited architects), built 1838–41, opened 1 March 1841, final cost ≈ £82,000 (vs. £37,500 budget) — "nearly twice" the Athenaeum's. Portland-stone Italian-palazzo front; central Saloon "cortile" 57 × 51 ft under a coved glazed roof with an oval saucer-dome, a two-storey peristyle "Ionic surmounted by Corinthian" (yellow marble/scagliola), tessellated pavement; the coffee-room (dining room) has "nine tall casement windows in the long south wall" onto the garden. Grade I listed. | high | [3][8][9] | Verne's "twenty red porphyry Ionic columns…dome…blue windows" is a garble; his "nine windows on the garden" is exact. |
| F-16 | Celebrity chef Alexis Soyer was the Reform's chef 1837–1850 and helped Barry plan the famous kitchens (1838), later publicised in his *Gastronomic Regenerator*. | high | [3][8] | Backs the "cosy Victorian-club warmth / food" mood. |
| F-17 | Visiting today: the Reform Club is members-only, but it opens for guided tours during the **Open House London festival — 12–13 September 2026**; bookings open at midday on **19 August 2026** (free account needed); tours last ~45 min and include "one very long staircase". | high (for 2026) | [4] | Time-sensitive: bookings open the day after this sheet's date. |
| F-18 | Pall Mall: ~0.6 km long, running east from St James's Street across Waterloo Place to the Haymarket; named after *pall-mall*, a 17th-century mallet-and-ball game (Italian *pallamaglio*); one of the first streets in London lit by gas after Frederick Winsor's demonstration on 4 June 1807. "Clubland": Travellers Club (No. 106, 1823 — Barry's first palazzo, next door to the Reform), Athenaeum (No. 107, 1830), Reform (104–105, 1836), Army and Navy (36–39, 1837), Oxford and Cambridge, Royal Automobile Club (No. 89). | high | [10] | The Athenaeum, Travellers and Reform stand in a row — one Street View pan covers all three. |
| F-19 | Savile Row was built 1731–35 on the Burlington Estate and named after Lady Dorothy Savile, wife of the 3rd Earl of Burlington. Tailors arrived from c. 1803; Henry Poole & Co (founded 1806, in the Row from 1846, now at No. 15) is called "the founding father of Savile Row" and made the Prince of Wales's short evening jacket that became the dinner jacket/tuxedo. In 1872 the Row was already tailoring territory. | high | [5][11] | Sensory: the Row still smells of cloth and chalk; the word "bespoke" is bound to it. |
| F-20 | Savile Row today: No. 14 (Sheridan's actual house) was Hardy Amies' HQ from 1946 until the shop closed in March 2019; Hackett London took the space in 2019. No. 3 was Apple Corps' HQ where the Beatles played their rooftop concert on 30 January 1969. The Savile Row Bespoke Association promotes "the best bespoke tailoring in the world for more than two centuries". | med (current occupants change) | [5][6][12][13] | Check Street View for current shopfronts before scripting. |
| F-21 | Charing Cross station opened **11 January 1864** (South Eastern Railway) on the site of Hungerford Market; single-span wrought-iron roof by Sir John Hawkshaw, 510 ft × 164 ft; the Charing Cross Hotel (E. M. Barry, 250 bedrooms) opened **15 May 1865**; the 70-ft Eleanor Cross replica in the forecourt (E. M. Barry, carved by Thomas Earp, Portland stone/Mansfield stone/Aberdeen granite) dates from 1865 and is Grade II* listed. In 1872 the station was eight years old — Fogg leaves from London's newest West End terminus. | high | [14][15] | The 1291 original cross stood where the Charles I statue (1675) now is; London distances are measured from that statue, not the replica. |
| F-22 | Charing Cross today: 6 platforms, managed by Network Rail, served by Southeastern with typically 1 train per hour to Dover Priory; 19.8 million passenger entries/exits in 2024–25; the roof collapsed in 1905 (six killed) and was replaced; the 1986–90 Embankment Place office block by Terry Farrell now sits over the platforms; the hotel is now **The Clermont London, Charing Cross** (rebranded from Amba, May 2021). Trains still leave over Hungerford Bridge, flanked by the Golden Jubilee footbridges. | high | [14][16] | You can still buy a ticket "for Dover" at Fogg's station — the through-Paris route now uses St Pancras. |
| F-23 | Fogg's bank, "Baring's" (his £20,000 is on deposit there and he was "recommended by the Barings" to the club, ch. I, III), was Barings Bank, one of London's oldest merchant banks (est. 1762, at 8 Bishopsgate from 1806); it collapsed in 1995 after Nick Leeson's £827 m trading losses. | high | [17] | Then-vs-now line: Fogg's bank no longer exists. |
| F-40 | Today a Southeastern train from London Charing Cross to Dover Priory takes about two hours: a real-time, unedited passenger recording of the run (2026) lasts 2 h 02 m 10 s from departure to arrival. | med (single recording; timetable not checked) | [46] | added-by:rundown-writer. Line for the departure beat: "the same run takes about two hours today". Confirm against the current Southeastern timetable before shipping. |

## C. London in 1872

| id | fact | confidence | sources | notes |
|----|------|-----------|---------|-------|
| F-24 | Population: the 1871 census counted 3,272,441 in Inner London (County of London area) and 3,902,178 in what is now Greater London — comfortably the largest city in the world at the time (the "largest city" claim is standard but not in this source). | high (numbers) / med (largest-city) | [18] | Humanise: "close to four million — more than today's Los Angeles city". |
| F-25 | The Metropolitan Railway — the world's first underground railway — opened 10 January 1863 (Paddington–Farringdon, 3.75 mi/6 km, steam locomotives, gas-lit wooden carriages, 38,000 passengers on day one); by October 1871 an "Inner Circle" service ran between Mansion House and Moorgate. So in 1872 London already had a (smoky) Underground. | high | [19] | Fogg pointedly does not use it. |
| F-26 | The Victoria Embankment (Bazalgette; built 1865–70) opened 13 July 1870: reclaimed foreshore carrying the low-level sewer and the District Railway; in December 1878 it became the first British street permanently lit by electricity. Big Ben: clock tower completed 31 May 1859, Great Bell first chimed 11 July 1859; renamed Elizabeth Tower in 2012; 96 m tall, bell 13.7 t; restored 2017–22 (£69 m). In 1872 both were brand-new landmarks. | high | [20][21] | Then/now photo pair candidate: Embankment from Hungerford Bridge. |
| F-27 | Reading sauce (in Fogg's breakfast) was a real Berkshire condiment: fishmonger James Cocks of Reading began selling it in 1802; ingredients walnut and mushroom ketchup, soy, anchovies, chillies, garlic; hugely popular in the Victorian period, exported to India from 1851; died out in the 1960s. | med | [22] | Souvenir candidate: "make a Reading-sauce-style fish supper". |
| F-28 | Cabs: the hansom (patented 1834 by Joseph Hansom, redesigned 1836 by John Chapman: two wheels, driver perched high behind, passengers speak through a roof hatch, folding doors over the knees) and the four-wheeled "growler". Fare: "6d. per mile, or part of a mile, for two persons, within a four miles radius of Charing Cross" (Cruchley's 1865); c. 4,300 cabs in London in 1865. Horse cabs were superseded by motor taxis from 1908; last London horse-cab licence surrendered 1947. | high | [23][24] | Fogg's cab from Savile Row to Charing Cross (~2.5 km) would have cost about a shilling. |
| F-29 | Gas: Fogg's house is lit and warmed by gas (F-03); Pall Mall had gas from 1807 (F-18). Today roughly 1,300 working gas street lamps survive in London, tended by British Gas lamplighters; Westminster had 275 in 2022 and proposed converting them to LED (contested by the GMB union). | med | [25][26] | Sensory: watch for the gas lamps on The Mall / Carlton House Terrace steps at dusk. |
| F-30 | Fog — is a "pea-souper" fair for 2 Oct 1872? Verne gives *rain* (F-13). Real London fogs were coal-smoke winter phenomena ("radiation weather"), brown/reddish-yellow/greenish with a "smoky, or sulphurous smell" (Rollo Russell, 1880); the notorious fog of **December 1873** killed prize cattle at the Smithfield Club show and pushed the week's deaths ~700 above average; fogs peaked in the 1880s. "Pea-soup" as a fog word is attested from 1820; "London particular" also meant a Madeira wine, later a pea-and-ham soup. | high | [27][28][29] | Verdict for the writer: an early-October evening should be **wet and dark, not foggy**; save the pea-souper for a "one year later" aside. |
| F-31 | Slang current in 1872 London (glossary printed in Dickens's *All the Year Round*, 17 Oct 1874): "Bloke — …to signify disrespectfully a man"; "Cove — a man"; "Brick — the highest commendation of a man's character"; "Beak — a magistrate"; "Crib — a house, a lodging"; "Hook it — be off!"; "Rhino — money". Money slang: "bob" = shilling, "tanner" = sixpence, "joey" = threepenny bit; "swell"/"toff" = a stylishly dressed gentleman. | high (AYR list) / med (money terms) | [32][34] | Fogg is a "swell" to a cabby; the beggar-woman gets twenty guineas — 420 bob. |

## D. Verne's real inspirations and the book itself

| id | fact | confidence | sources | notes |
|----|------|-----------|---------|-------|
| F-32 | Publication: serialised in *Le Temps* from 6 November to 22 December 1872 — the ending timed so Fogg's real-calendar deadline (21 Dec) fell as the last instalments appeared; book edition by Hetzel 30 January 1873; George M. Towle's English translation 1873. Verne was born Nantes 8 Feb 1828, died Amiens 24 March 1905; his first trip abroad (1859) was by sea to Liverpool and Scotland with Aristide Hignard. | high | [35][36][37] | Serial ran while the fictional journey was "happening" — a nice hook for a persona. |
| F-33 | The three enablers Verne's characters cite: Suez Canal opened 17 Nov 1869 (193 km, cutting ~8,900 km off London–Arabian Sea voyages); the US transcontinental railroad completed 10 May 1869 at Promontory Summit ("days instead of months" coast to coast); the Bombay–Allahabad–Calcutta through rail route opened 7 March 1870 when the GIPR met the East Indian Railway at Jabalpur — Verne mis-routes it via Aurangabad, and his "Rothal" is unidentified. | high | [38][39][40] | Feeds the "the world has grown smaller" line ("It was once," said Fogg). |
| F-34 | An 1870 article in *Le Magasin pittoresque* had already argued a round-the-world trip in 80 days was possible since Suez opened — a likely seed of the *Daily Telegraph* table in the novel. | med | [41] | Search-snippet source only; treat as "often cited". |
| F-35 | Thomas Cook led the first commercial round-the-world tour in 1872 — away 222 days (Liverpool 26 Sept 1872 → New York → across America → Yokohama → Asia → home), nearly 30,000 miles, reporting in letters to *The Times* — i.e. Cook was mid-Atlantic while Verne's serial started. | high (222 days, 1872) / med (26 Sept Liverpool) | [42][43] | Cook and Verne's Fogg went in opposite directions (Cook west, Fogg east). |
| F-36 | George Francis Train (American) left New York 10 July 1870, sailed from San Francisco 1 Aug, reached Yokohama 26 Aug and Marseille 22 Oct, was jailed in Lyon, and got home 21 Dec 1870 — 164 days, but he claimed 80 "travelling" days and later said "Verne stole my thunder. I'm Phileas Fogg." He later went round in 67½ days (1890) and 60 days (1892). | high | [44] | Note: home on 21 December — the same date as Fogg's deadline, two years earlier. |
| F-37 | Bradshaw: George Bradshaw published the world's first compiled railway timetable on 19 Oct 1839 (sixpence); *Bradshaw's Continental Railway Guide* began June 1847 and grew past 1,000 pages of timetables, guide and hotel lists — the "red-bound" book under Fogg's arm. | high | [45] | Prop for the illustration budget. |
| F-38 | Verne also acknowledged Edgar Allan Poe's "Three Sundays in a Week" (1841) — a round-the-world day-gain story ending in a marriage — as an influence; and the American traveller William Perry Fogg published world-tour letters in 1872. | med | [35] | Only if a "where did Fogg's name come from" aside is wanted. |

## Suggested angles for the writer
- **Verne got the address wrong — and it doesn't matter.** No. 7 vs No. 14, 1814 vs 1816 (F-01). Open on Savile Row with the Sheridan plaque and let the traveller "correct" Verne; it sets the tone: the novel is a guidebook, not scripture.
- **Count the steps.** 1,151 steps from Savile Row to the Reform (F-05) is close to the real distance. A Street View walk that literally counts to 1,151 is a ready-made interaction and a Fogg-precision joke.
- **The nine windows are real.** Verne's Reform Club is half fantasy (red porphyry, blue glass) and half exact (nine windows on the garden, two libraries, famous tea) — F-07 vs F-15. Good "then vs now" quiz: which detail did Verne invent?
- **The wager as arithmetic.** 1,920 hours; 115,200 minutes; 21 December really is 80 days later and really was a Saturday (F-11). Fogg's mottoes ("The unforeseen does not exist") make a strong overlay caption (≤ 12 words).
- **The world had just shrunk.** Suez 1869, Promontory 1869, Jabalpur 1870 (F-33) — three dates within 10 months, two years before the story. Map beat: light the three links, then draw the 80-day loop (F-10).
- **Two real men were "doing it" as Verne wrote.** Cook was crossing America westward while the serial ran (F-35); Train got home on 21 December 1870 (F-36). Verne persona can say, in biography, that Train later claimed to be Fogg.
- **Rain, not fog.** The departure night is dark and wet (F-13); a pea-souper would be a cliché and slightly wrong for October (F-30). Sound design: rain on a station roof, steam, a whistle at 8:45.
- **Fogg's newest London.** Charing Cross was eight years old, the Underground nine, the Embankment two, Big Ben thirteen (F-21, F-25, F-26). Fogg's London is modern, not olde-worlde — a useful corrective to the audience's expectation.
- **The beggar-woman.** Twenty guineas (= 420 shillings, or ~840 cab-miles at 6d) handed over without breaking stride (F-12, F-28) — the one warm beat before the whistle.
- **Souvenir candidates.** (a) A club-breakfast menu you can cook: broiled fish + a Reading-sauce-style walnut/mushroom ketchup (F-06, F-27); (b) the Open House 2026 booking date, 19 Aug — the one weekend a year you can stand in Fogg's Saloon (F-17); (c) a slang card: bloke, cove, brick, bob, tanner (F-31).
- **Cockney/Victorian slang** — keep to the 1874 All the Year Round list (F-31) for authenticity; treat "toff/swell" as gentleman-spotting words from the cabby's rank.
- **Answering the founder's open question:** the text supports ending Day 1 either at the platform (8:45 whistle) or at Sydenham with the forgotten gas (F-13); the latter is a laugh line and a natural "to be continued".
