## A. Savile Row — why one short street owns the word "suit"

| id | fact | confidence | sources | notes |
|----|------|-----------|---------|-------|
| F-110 | **The whole street is 280 m long.** Measured end to end from OpenStreetMap way geometry (ways 4259249 + 4370945 + 18791974, Conduit Street in the north to Vigo Street / Burlington Gardens in the south): **280 m**, a three-minute walk, one block behind Regent Street. Built 1731–35 on the Burlington estate as ordinary gentry housing (see F-19). | high (OSM geometry; ±10 m) | [P38] | The founder's complaint answered with a number: this is a street you can walk in three minutes, and the world's word for a made-to-order anything comes off it. 📷 A single unbroken walking shot can cover the *entire* subject — rare and worth using. |
| F-111 | **Why tailoring landed here, and not somewhere else.** Three things converged. (1) The Burlington estate was laid out for the gentry; Savile Row's first residents were army officers and politicians. (2) Tailors were already one street west, in **Cork Street from c. 1790**, dressing Beau Brummell's London; they crossed into Savile Row from **c. 1803**, and Henry Poole opened a front door on the Row in **1846**. (3) The trade simply followed its customers — the court at St James's (F-143), the officers, and the Mayfair houses. Nothing about the street itself is special; the *customers* were. | high | [P2][P3] | This is the honest answer to "why here", and it is more interesting than a myth: a trade cluster, like Silicon Valley, formed by proximity to buyers and to each other. |
| F-112 | **The root of the trade is uniform, not fashion.** The two firms now at No. 1 began as military outfitters: **Hawkes, 1771** (army caps and helmets — it supplied Wellington's army), and **Gieves, 1785** (Admiralty and Royal Navy; it claims the naval pea coat). They merged in 1974 and moved into No. 1 in 1975. The Savile Row silhouette — structured shoulder, waisted, high armhole — descends from clothes cut for men who had to ride, march and be seen at a distance. | high (dates) / med (silhouette lineage — widely stated, not proven by these sources) | [P8][P9] | 📷 Military tailoring gives you pictures: uniform plates, regimental coats, a cutter's shears. |
| F-113 | **No. 1 Savile Row was the Royal Geographical Society's headquarters from 1870 to 1912** — the room where British expeditions to Africa, Asia and the South Pole were planned. **David Livingstone's body lay there in April 1874** before burial in Westminster Abbey. On **23 December 1912 Hawkes & Co bought the building from the RGS for £38,000**, partly because the firm had dressed so many of the explorers who had planned their journeys inside it. | high | [P3][P8] | The single most on-theme fact on this street for a travel product: in **1872**, the year Fogg is supposed to be living six doors away, No. 1 was the actual headquarters of British exploration. 📷 Blue plaque + RGS engravings + the shopfront today. |
| F-114 | **Where "bespoke" really comes from.** From the verb **bespeak**, "to order (goods)", attested in that sense from the **1580s**; Old English *besprecan*, a common Germanic compound originally meaning "to call out". The **adjective** *bespoke*, for goods made to order as opposed to ready-made, is attested from **1755** (OED's earliest evidence). | high | [P4][P5] | The word means "spoken for" — the folklore is right about the *sense* and wrong about the *place* (F-115). |
| F-115 | ⚠ **FOLKLORE — "bespoke" did not originate on Savile Row.** The story repeated by tailors, guidebooks and even Wikipedia is that the word began here, when a bolt of cloth was said to "be spoken for" by a customer. The dating refutes it: the ordering sense of *bespeak* is **1580s** and the adjective is **1755**, while tailors only reached Savile Row about **1803** — the word is roughly fifty years older than the first tailor on the street and two centuries older in its root sense. No dictionary traces it to the street. **Treat as trade legend; never narrate as fact.** | high (that the dating refutes the strong claim) | [P2][P4][P5] | This is a *better* beat than the myth, and exactly the "Verne got the address wrong and it doesn't matter" move that already works in Day 1. |
| F-116 | **What the trade actually involves — the Savile Row Bespoke Association standard.** Every suit must be **hand-made**, cut from a **paper pattern made for one person**; jacket foreparts **entirely hand-canvassed**; buttonholes sewn, sleeves set and linings felled **by hand**; a minimum of **50 hours of hand labour** in a two-piece suit; **three intermediate fittings**; about **three months** from commission to finished garment; and everything must be made **within 100 yards of Savile Row**, overseen by a **Master Cutter**. | high (trade body's own published standard) | [P1][P2] | The 100-yard rule is the whole argument in one line: the standard is defined by *geography*. 📷 Chalk on cloth, shears, a basted half-made jacket, the fitting-room mirror. |
| F-117 | **How long it takes to make the maker.** An apprentice trains **two to six years** under a Savile Row house to specialise in **one** part of the job — coat-making, trouser-making, or cutting — before being appraised by a panel of master tailors. The SRBA apprenticeship scheme launched in **2007** and has graduated **over 100** young people. | high | [P1] | Humanising number for narration: six years to be trusted with *trousers*. |
| F-118 | **What it costs.** **Huntsman (11 Savile Row) quotes bespoke commissions from £7,500** for a two-piece, minimum three fittings, made on the premises. Other houses start lower (widely quoted £4,000–£6,000 range in 2025 press); £7,500 is a first-party figure and safe to say. | high (Huntsman's own site, 2026-09-08) / low (the "other houses" range — press, not first-party) | [P7] | Say the price. It is the fact that makes 50 hours of hand-sewing real. |
| F-119 | **The trade has shrunk by an order of magnitude.** The Association of London Master Tailors listed **169 member firms in 1912**; there were about **40 tailoring businesses** on and around the Row in the **1950s**; **19 by 2006**. In **2019** the SRBA reported **19 tailoring houses, about 400 tailors employed and roughly £40 million** of annual revenue. Today the SRBA lists **22 member and associate member houses**. | high (1912, 2006, 2019, 2026 figures) / med (the 1950s "about 40") | [P1][P2] | 169 → 19 in a century is the shape of the story. Note the two counts measure different things (Association members London-wide in 1912 vs. houses on the Row) — say "firms in the trade" not "shops on the street" for 1912. |
| F-120 | **The pressure is rent, and the defence is planning law.** The SRBA fought Abercrombie & Fitch's children's store at **No. 3** in **2012**; the shop opened anyway in **2013** and closed in **2021**. In **November 2016 Westminster City Council designated Savile Row a Special Policy Area** — planning protection written specifically to keep bespoke tailoring on the street against chain retail. In **2025** SRBA chairman **William Skinner** said rents were "very high"; the **Pollen Estate**, the dominant landlord, appointed new retail leasing agents in April 2025 and has considered rent-free periods and deferrals. | high (SPA 2016, A&F dates) / med (2025 rent situation — trade press) | [P2][P3][P11] | The live tension: a craft kept alive partly by a council planning designation. 📷 Shopfront vs. chain-store fascia. |
| F-121 | ⚠ **"Bespoke" is not a protected word — and Savile Row lost the case.** In **June 2008** the Advertising Standards Authority dismissed a complaint (backed by the Row's tailors) against **Sartoriani**, which advertised **£495** suits as "bespoke" although, after a London fitting, the cloth was cut and largely machine-sewn in Germany. The ASA held that **"bespoke" and "made-to-measure" are effectively synonymous in modern usage**, and that most consumers would not expect a bespoke garment to be entirely hand-made. That ruling is *why* the Row now trades on the defined "Savile Row Bespoke" standard in F-116 rather than on the word alone. | med-high (press reports of the adjudication; the ASA adjudication text itself was not fetched) | [P6][P2] | The arc for the film: the word's real origin (F-114) → the street's myth about it (F-115) → the street losing the word in a tribunal (F-121) → the 100-yard rule invented to replace it (F-116). That is a *place* story with a beginning, middle and end, and Verne is nowhere in it. |
| F-122 | **The Row is not frozen.** **Kathryn Sargent** became Savile Row's **first female head cutter in 2009** (at Gieves & Hawkes, where she had worked since the mid-1990s), and on **6 April 2016** became the first woman to open her own shop on Savile Row. | high | [P10] | 📷 A living face for a trade otherwise shown as sepia. Good counterweight to a street that can look like a museum. |

## B. The Reform Club — and what a London club actually *was*

| id | fact | confidence | sources | notes |
|----|------|-----------|---------|-------|
| F-123 | **What a gentlemen's club was for.** A private, members-only house descended from the 18th-century coffee and chocolate houses — **White's, 1693, is the oldest** — which took over the coffee houses' social role. It was a **second home**: dining room, library, billiard room, writing room, quiet rooms, and in many clubs **bedrooms**, all staffed with servants. A member could eat, read the papers, write letters, receive post, meet a colleague and sleep, without ever inviting anyone into his actual house. That is the point: it gave a man with a modest London address the run of a palace. | high (facilities, White's 1693) / med (the "palace on a subscription" framing — widely made, inferred here) | [P12][P13] | This is the answer to the founder's question about the Reform. Verne's Fogg — a man with no study, no books and no visitors (F-03) who spends his whole day at the club — is a *documentary* portrait of the institution, not an eccentricity. 📷 Interiors: Fagan plates M-96/M-100/M-101 already in the manifest. |
| F-124 | **Who could join: you did not apply, you were elected.** Candidates were **proposed and seconded** by existing members who had known them for years, then **balloted** by the membership; a set proportion of black balls excluded the candidate — the origin of **"blackballed"**. The club was a filter, not a business selling access. Concrete illustration: **Winston Churchill resigned from the Reform Club in 1913** in protest at the blackballing of a friend. | high (procedure; Churchill 1913) / low (the exact black-ball ratio — commonly given as one in ten, **not verified in this pass**) | [P12][P13][P14] | Do not state a ratio on screen. "One black ball in ten" is everywhere and sourced nowhere I checked. |
| F-125 | **Clubland was an entire parallel institution, not a handful of famous doors.** At the height of their influence in the **late 19th century London had over 400 clubs**; of the roughly 200 principal clubs standing at the turn of the 20th century, about **half had been founded in the previous thirty years**. They clustered in **St James's and Pall Mall**, mostly in purpose-built or converted palazzi. | med (two secondary sources give 400+ and ~200 respectively, counting different things) | [P12][P13] | Say "hundreds", not a precise number. The *shape* is the fact: a boom, concentrated in a few streets. |
| F-126 | **Clubs sorted men by allegiance and calling, not just by class.** White's (Tory), Brooks's (Whig), Boodle's (country gentlemen), the **Carlton** (Conservative, 1832/33), the **Reform** (Radical and Whig, 1836), the **Athenaeum** (men of distinction in science, literature and the arts, 1830), and — next door to the Reform — the **Travellers**, founded 1819 "to form a point of re-union for gentlemen who had travelled abroad; and to afford them the opportunity of inviting, as Honorary Visitors, the principal members of all the foreign missions and travellers of distinction." | high (the Travellers' stated object, quoted from the Survey of London) / high (club affiliations) | [P39][P12][P13] | The Travellers' founding object is the single best line in this section for *our* product — a club whose purpose was people who had come back from somewhere. It stands three doors from the Reform (F-18). ⚠ The famous rule that a Travellers candidate must have gone **500 miles from London in a straight line** is repeated everywhere; the Survey of London page does **not** contain it. Unverified — do not narrate. |
| F-127 | **Why the Reform Club exists at all: it is a political weapon.** After the Reform Act 1832 and the general election of December 1834, reformers wanted "an Association or Club (call it what you will) in London to counter the machinations of the Tory **Carlton Club**" (founded 1832/33 under Wellington and Peel, and then at 94 Pall Mall, a few doors away). A provisional committee first met at **Edward Ellice's** house in Carlton House Terrace on **8 February 1836**; Ellice was a Whig whip. The club opened at **104 Pall Mall in May 1836** with about **1,000 members, including nearly 250 MPs**. In the 1830s a club did the work a party headquarters does now — registration of voters, whipping, candidate selection. | high (Ellice, 1836, Carlton rivalry) / med (the 1,000 members / 250 MPs figures, from the club's own history document read via search snippet) | [P14][P15][P16] | Extends F-14. This is what makes the Reform *interesting* rather than merely old: it is a political party that happened to be built as a palace. |
| F-128 | **What it is now.** Politically unaligned since the Liberal Party's decline after the Second World War; about **2,700 members**, of whom roughly **500 are overseas members and over 400 are women**; a members' **library of more than 85,000 volumes** built by donation. The Reform was **one of the first traditional gentlemen's clubs to admit women on equal terms, in 1981**. Members have included Thackeray, Henry James, Arnold Bennett, Lloyd George and Churchill. | high | [P14] | Refines F-14 (adds the library, the overseas/women split, the literary members). |
| F-129 | **Clubland today.** Most of the historic London clubs now admit women; a few have not, and the question is still contested inside them. The buildings survive largely intact in the same few hundred metres of Pall Mall and St James's Street, which is why the street frontage a viewer sees today is close to what Fogg would have seen. | med (general; specific club-by-club policies **not verified in this pass**) | [P12][P13] | Do not name which clubs still exclude women without checking. The safe, filmable claim is about the *buildings*, and it is true. 📷 One slow pan down Pall Mall covers Travellers, Reform and Athenaeum (F-18). |

## C. Charing Cross — the point London is measured from

| id | fact | confidence | sources | notes |
|----|------|-----------|---------|-------|
| F-130 | ⚠ **What "Charing" means — and the myth.** Charing was a hamlet on the road between the City and Westminster, "little more than wayside cottages serving the Royal Mews". The name is **Old English *cierring*, "a turning" or "a bend"** — of the Thames, or (per A. D. Mills, *A Dictionary of London Place Names*) more likely of the old Roman road running west. The much-loved derivation from French ***chère reine*, "dear queen"**, for Eleanor of Castile, is a **folk etymology**: the place name pre-dates Eleanor's death (1290) by at least a century. | high (OE origin and the debunking) / med (Mills's road-bend preference, cited via secondary sources) | [P17][P36] | A one-line myth-buster with a picture: the bend is still there in the road. |
| F-131 | **What the Eleanor crosses were — a fact worth having whether or not you care about Verne.** **Eleanor of Castile died 28 November 1290 at Harby, Nottinghamshire.** Her body was carried about **180 miles to Westminster Abbey over 12 days**. Edward I raised **twelve stone crosses, one at each place the bier rested overnight**, built 1291–c.1295, each inscribed *Orate pro anima* — "pray for her soul" — so that passing travellers would pray as they passed. They are, in effect, the ancestor of every roadside memorial. **Only three survive**: Geddington, Hardingstone (Northampton) and Waltham Cross. | high | [P18] | 📷 The strongest map beat available in Day 1 that has nothing to do with Fogg: animate the funeral route north-to-south, dropping twelve crosses, then dim the nine that are gone. |
| F-132 | **Charing was the last stop before the Abbey, and the most expensive cross of the twelve** — **over £700**, against roughly **£100–£120** for the others. Master masons **Richard and Roger of Crundale**; Eleanor's figures carved by **Alexander of Abingdon** from Caen stone. | high | [P18] | Why the biggest cross is at *this* corner: it was the last night of a royal funeral. |
| F-133 | **Destroyed by Parliament.** Demolition was ordered in **1643**; the cross actually came down in **1647**, having stood **353 years**. Parish accounts of 1657–58 record its stone being sold and reused — "47 loads of stone for the parishes use for paving", and £77 1s. 5d. received "from stones dug up and sould". | high | [P19][P18] | ⚠ Note a live discrepancy: the Wikipedia *Eleanor cross* article gives "2 May 1643" for the demolition. The Survey of London (ordered 1643, down 1647) is the better authority. Use **1647**, or say "ordered down in 1643 and demolished in 1647". |
| F-134 | **What stands on the spot now.** The **equestrian bronze of Charles I**, cast by **Hubert Le Sueur in 1633**, ordered broken up under the Commonwealth, hidden instead by the brazier **John Rivet**, bought back by the Crown and erected at Charing Cross by order of **April 1675**, finished by 1676, on a pedestal designed by **Christopher Wren** and carved by Joshua Marshall. It stands on the site of the medieval cross. | high | [P19] | 📷 It faces down Whitehall towards the place Charles I was executed — a fact the picture makes for you. |
| F-135 | **This is the spot London is measured from.** Since the early 19th century Charing Cross has been the notional **centre of London** and the datum for **road distances to London**; legislation of the early 1800s used it to define how far "London" extended. When a road sign in Scotland says "London 400", it means *this statue*. **Important:** the datum is the **statue**, not the Victorian replica cross in the station forecourt — E. M. Barry's 1863–65 replica stands about **200 yards** away. | high (datum at Charing Cross; replica ~200 yards off site) | [P17][P19][P20] | Corrects a natural mistake the film could easily make. 📷 The small plaque by the statue; then a road sign with a mileage to London. |
| F-136 | **Why anyone put a railway station here.** The South Eastern Railway's only London terminus was **London Bridge** — the wrong side of the river for the West End. Its secretary **Samuel Smiles** identified the site of the old **Hungerford Market** beside the Strand as the best available; the **Charing Cross Railway Act 1859** authorised the line, the SER put in **£300,000** of capital, work began in **June 1860**, a trial run ran on **1 December 1863** and the station opened **11 January 1864**. It is the closest main-line terminus to the centre of London — because the centre of London is defined 100 metres from its front door (F-135). | high | [P20] | Extends F-21 with the *reason*, which is what the founder is asking for. |
| F-137 | **The bridge the train leaves over is a recycled Brunel — and half of it is now in Bristol.** Brunel's **Hungerford suspension footbridge** opened in **1845**; the railway bought it in **1859** and took it down to build John Hawkshaw's rail bridge into the new station. Its **suspension chains were sold for £5,000 and re-used in 1863 to complete the Clifton Suspension Bridge** in Bristol, whose construction had stalled for want of money (a third chain was added). So the bridge out of Charing Cross and the most famous bridge in Bristol are two halves of the same object. | high (chains reused at Clifton, 1863) / med (the exact £5,000 figure — secondary sources) | [P21][P22] | 📷 The best cheap visual in the whole chapter: an engraving of the old Hungerford footbridge, a photograph of Clifton, a train leaving Charing Cross today. Three shots, one idea, no narration needed after the first sentence. |

## D. London in 1872 — why a bet like this starts here

| id | fact | confidence | sources | notes |
|----|------|-----------|---------|-------|
| F-138 | **The scale, in comparisons a viewer can hold.** London had been the **largest city in the world since about 1825** and still was in 1872. **By the 1860s it was a quarter larger than the world's second city, Beijing; two-thirds larger than Paris; and five times larger than New York.** Population: over **1 million in 1801** → **3.9 million** (the modern Greater London area) at the **1871 census** → **5.567 million by 1891**. | high (census figures) / med (the 1860s comparative sentence, from a secondary source) | [P23] | Extends F-24 with the comparisons, which is what makes the number mean anything. 📷 A bar-race or three simple silhouettes — but per D9, **big type, few labels, revealed over time**. |
| F-139 | **The port.** The Port of London handled **800,000 tons of goods a year in 1800 and 8 million tons by 1880** — a tenfold increase in eighty years — and was the **world's largest port**. | high | [P23] | The reason the steamship timetables in Fogg's *Bradshaw* all radiate from here. 📷 Docks engravings; forests of masts. |
| F-140 | **The railways.** By **1865 London had twelve principal railway termini** — Euston (1837), Paddington (1838), Fenchurch Street (1841), Waterloo (1848), King's Cross (1852), Charing Cross (1864), St Pancras (1868) among them. Fogg's London was a *modern* city and looked it — new stone, new iron, new gas. | high (derived from F-21/F-25/F-26; no new source) | — | Corrective for the audience's default picture of "Victorian London" as ancient and grimy. ## E. Pall Mall and St James's — why this quarter, briefly

| id | fact | confidence | sources | notes |
|----|------|-----------|---------|-------|
| F-143 | **The quarter exists because the King moved in.** **St James's Palace**, built by Henry VIII from **1536**, became the monarch's **principal London residence in 1698**, after fire destroyed the **Palace of Whitehall**. It remained the sovereign's official seat even after Victoria moved to Buckingham Palace in **1837**, and is still the administrative centre of the court. | high | [P26] | One sentence explains the entire neighbourhood: palace → courtiers → houses → shops → clubs. 📷 The Tudor gatehouse on Pall Mall — it is still there, and it is the oldest thing in the shot. |
| F-144 | **Which is why every ambassador to Britain is accredited to "the Court of St James's"** — the formal name of the royal court, used in every set of diplomatic credentials, naming a palace that has not housed a reigning monarch for nearly two centuries. | high | [P27] | Pure general knowledge, and the kind a viewer repeats at dinner. Cheap to say, costs four seconds. |
| F-145 | **And why the trades clustered.** After the Restoration of 1660, courtiers and aristocrats built houses to be near the palace; shops grew along **St James's Street**, which runs straight north from the palace gate, to dress and feed them. Survivors: **Lock & Co, hatters, No. 6 St James's Street since 1676** (the world's oldest hat shop); **Berry Bros & Rudd, wine, from 1698**; **John Lobb, bootmaker, 1866**. Clubland settled into the same few hundred metres for the same reason. Savile Row (F-111) is the same phenomenon, one quarter-mile north. | med-high (founding dates widely and consistently reported; not first-party checked in this pass) | [P28] | The unifying idea of Day 1's walk: **Fogg's route from Savile Row to Pall Mall is a walk through the supply chain of a Victorian gentleman** — clothes, then club, then station. ## F. Dover and Calais — the two ports facing each other (Day 2 material, gathered now)

| id | fact | confidence | sources | notes |
|----|------|-----------|---------|-------|
| F-146 | **The narrowest place.** The **Strait of Dover** is the narrowest part of the English Channel: **33.2 km (20.6 miles)** at its narrowest, between **South Foreland**, near Dover, and **Cap Gris-Nez**, near Calais. It is shallow — about **46 m average**, **68 m maximum**. On a clear day each country is visible from the other. | high | [P29] | The founder's own example of "general knowledge worth having". Say the number; it is small enough to be surprising. |
| F-147 | **Why there is a strait at all — and why both sides are white.** Britain was joined to Europe by a chalk ridge, the **Weald–Artois anticline**. It was breached by **catastrophic floods**, when an ice-dammed lake in the southern North Sea overflowed: once about **425,000 years ago**, again about **225,000 years ago**. Rising sea level finally drowned the remaining land bridge around **6500–6200 BC**. **The white cliffs of Dover and the white cliffs at Cap Blanc-Nez are the two cut ends of the same ridge.** | high (the megaflood account and dates as given by the source) / med (the "same ridge, two ends" framing — a fair reading, not a quoted sentence) | [P29] | 📷 **The strongest picture in Day 2.** A ridge, a flood, a channel — an animated map that a five-year-old could follow, over a shot of the cliffs. Pairs with the existing chalk/Cretaceous fact (Day 2 F-77). |
| F-148 | **It is the busiest shipping lane in the world.** Over **400 commercial vessels a day** pass through the strait (some sources say 500–600), in through-traffic running northeast and southwest — and that stream is crossed **at right angles** by the Dover–Calais and Dover–Dunkirk ferries. It is among the most closely traffic-managed waters on earth. | med (400+ is the conservative figure; higher counts come from weaker sources) | [P29][P37] | 📷 An AIS/marine-traffic style plot of one day's shipping is a gift: a river of dots crossed by a stitching of ferries. Check licensing before using any live traffic site's imagery. |
| F-149 | **Why the route has always run here.** Dover has the **shortest crossing** and a natural harbour at the mouth of the river Dour. **Caesar sailed from Portus Itius in 55 BC and steered for Dubris (Dover)** — and was driven off, because the Britons massed on the cliffs could throw javelins down onto anyone landing. The Romans nonetheless made Dubris a military and cross-channel harbour and one of the **two starting points of Watling Street**, the road to London. ## Folklore watchlist — repeated everywhere, sourced nowhere
Anyone writing or checking Day 1 and Day 2 should treat these as **false or unproven** unless somebody finds a real source:
1. ⚠ **"Bespoke came from Savile Row / from cloth being 'spoken for' here."** The word is older than the street's tailors by ~50 years (F-114, F-115). The *sense* is right; the *place* is invented.
2. ⚠ **"Charing" from *chère reine*, "dear queen".** Folk etymology; the name predates Eleanor by a century (F-130).
3. ⚠ **"One black ball in ten excludes."** The blackball procedure is real; the ratio quoted is not sourced here (F-124).
4. ⚠ **The Travellers Club's "500 miles from London in a straight line" rule.** Not in the Survey of London's account of the club (F-126).
5. ⚠ **Dover Castle, "the Key of England."** Not in English Heritage's own history text (F-151).
6. ⚠ **Mary I's "Calais lying in my heart."** Holinshed, 1587, a generation after the event; generally treated as apocryphal (F-153).
7. Carried over from `fact-sheet.md`: **the dinner jacket / 1865 Prince of Wales** story is Henry Poole's own account (F-19, F-45), not independent history.

## Suggested angles for the writer
- **Savile Row as a complete three-act story with no Verne in it.** The word's real origin → the street's myth about the word → the street losing the word at the ASA in 2008 → the 100-yard rule invented to replace it. Ends on a live question: can planning law keep a craft alive? (F-114, F-115, F-121, F-116, F-120.)
- **"Why here?" has the same answer three times in Day 1.** Tailors clustered by Savile Row because the customers were in Mayfair; shops and clubs clustered in St James's because the court was at the palace; the station was built at Charing Cross because that is where London is measured from. Fogg's walk is a walk through **a Victorian gentleman's supply chain** — clothes, club, train. Say that once, early, and every place beat afterwards is load-bearing. (F-111, F-143, F-145, F-136.)
- **The Reform Club is a political party that got built as a palace.** Founded to out-organise the Carlton, opened with 250 MPs in it, and it is *because* of that origin that Fogg's whist table is full of bankers and engineers. (F-127, F-123, F-08.)
- **A club is what you have instead of a home.** Fogg has no study, no books and no visitors (F-03) and spends every waking hour at the Reform. Read the club as an institution first, and Fogg stops being an eccentric and becomes a *type*. (F-123.)
- **The Eleanor crosses are a love story told in twelve monuments** — and a piece of general knowledge most viewers do not have: twelve overnight stops, twelve crosses, three left. Fogg's departure point is a queen's last night on the road. (F-131, F-132.)
- **1872 is the year the world got wired.** Open or close the chapter here: the wager only makes sense in the one decade when you could *move* around the world in eighty days but *message* around it in minutes. (F-141, and F-33 in the main sheet.)
- **Dover and Calais: an animation, not a sentence.** A ridge, two floods, a channel, twenty miles, four hundred ships a day, a third of a nation's trade, and a tunnel back under the chalk. (F-146…F-155.)
- **Careful with "the centre of London".** The datum is the **statue**, not the pretty replica cross outside the station — a small correction that makes the film feel exact rather than touristic. (F-135.)
