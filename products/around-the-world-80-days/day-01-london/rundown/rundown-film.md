## 0 · What changed, in one paragraph

The old chapter was a walk through a novel: places appeared because Verne put something there, and the guide talked
almost continuously over stills. This film is a walk through **three places in half a mile of London**, each worth
twenty minutes on its own — a 280-metre street that owns the word "suit", an institution that let a man with a small
house live in a palace, and the paving stone Britain measures its road distances from — set in the one year, **1872**,
when the planet finished wiring itself shut. Phileas Fogg is the thread: teased at 0:27, and the story proper starts
at **5:00**, after the street has earned him. He stays only where he pays.

**The single strongest fact we own is F-141** (the Australian Overland Telegraph closed the last gap on 22 August
1872, six weeks before Fogg leaves). It reframes the whole wager: he races the ships, but the news about him travels
instantly. It is teased in the cold open and paid off in full as segment S8, the hinge of the film.

## 1 · The laws this film is cut to

Hard constraints for the Scene Developer and the Engine; every beat below already obeys them.

**L0 · The interestingness test (governing, `video-first.md`).** For every beat: *would a viewer who has never read
the book remember this tomorrow, and want to tell someone?* True is necessary, not sufficient. There is **no
place/novel ratio**. Nothing in this film is justified by "it is in the novel". Beats that survive on the novel's
side survive because they carry a **fact, a story or a lesson** — the world's first compiled railway timetable, a
bank brought down by one trader, a word that a tribunal took away — not because they explain a plot or a character.

**L1 · Shots, not sentences.** A picture changes when the eye tires, not when a sentence ends.
- **No still stays on screen longer than 12 s** without a cut or a slow move (push / pan across a detail).
- **Motion shots run 8–20 s**, archive film up to 20 s, and nothing runs past 30 s.
- Beats that hold three plates say so ("3 cuts") — the *beat* is 18 s, the *shots* are 6 s each.
- **88 beats, ~110 individual shots, in 1,134 s**: a visual change every **≈ 10 s**, and never longer than 20.

**L2 · Narration budget — the fix for "heavily narrated".**
- **≤ 1,600 words for the whole film** (≈ 60 % fill: the guide speaks about 10½ minutes of 18¾).
  Per-segment caps are in each header. If a beat cannot be said inside its cap it loses a fact, not a second of picture.
- **19 marked silences** (**HOLD** in the tables). They are content, not gaps (`positioning.md`, "the chill").
- Nothing spoken over the telegraph reveal's last 8 s, the whistle, or the beggar-woman.

**L3 · Text on screen — no floating anything (D9).** Only two legal forms:
1. **Burned captions** (the subtitle track) — they already do a lower-third's job.
2. **Designed type**: a **full-frame card** (cut to it, like a title page) or type *drawn into* a map. One exception,
   flagged for approval: an **opaque full-width band** locked to the bottom edge, used only for the three cold-open
   place names, the title, and — now mandatory — **the honest date on archive film**. It is opaque, static and lasts
   the whole shot; it does not hover, animate or point. If vetoed, those seven uses become full-frame cards at a cost
   of ~6 s. No pins. No lower-thirds. Ever.

**L4 · Map spec (D9).** Big type, **four labels maximum on screen at once**, high contrast, revealed over time,
legible on a 6-inch phone at arm's length. Every map here draws itself; none is presented complete.

**L5 · Quiz spec (D9).** **Pose → hold → reveal.** Options never render with the answer marked. **One quiz** (S2),
about a word rather than the novel; the guide states the answer only in the reveal (style guide R5).

**L6 · House look, protected (D9).** Period sound design and historic prints/engravings are the spine, not the
fallback: 24 of the 88 beats are engravings, plates or period photographs, and the sound bed never drops out.

**L7 · Truth on screen (founder, rev 2).** Archive film is **captioned with its own date** — "London, 1903",
"Trafalgar Square, 1890" — and the guide says out loud that it is thirty years late. We never let a picture imply a
year it does not have. The same rule governs the two folklore beats (§7): the legend is named as legend, on screen,
before it is refuted.

**L8 · Voice.** One guide, `shared/personas/guide.md`, clear English only (D8). The optional second voice
(Passepartout) is **cut in rev 2** — see §4b.

## 2 · Shape

**Hook (first 30 seconds):** three real places in half a mile, each stated as a fact a stranger would want, then a
world map wiring itself shut in August 1872 — *then* the man who bet half his fortune on that year. The title lands
at **0:36**, after the reason to stay.

**Three acts.**
- **Act I — The street (S1–S3, 5:44).** One 280-metre street owns a word, lost that word in a tribunal in 2008, and
  answered by defining the word with a tape measure. At the bottom of it sat the headquarters of British exploration
  — until a military tailor bought the building, because it had dressed the explorers who planned their journeys in it.
- **Act II — The institution (S4–S7, 5:56).** Why the quarter exists (a palace), what a club actually *was* (a second
  home you were elected into), why *this* club exists (a political weapon built as a palazzo), and the wager.
  **The turn:** "The train leaves for Dover at a quarter before nine."
- **Act III — The circuit (S8–S11, 7:14).** Why 1872 and not 1852: a port, twelve termini, an underground railway,
  a street lit by electricity — and a wire that closed round the planet six weeks before he left. Then the place he
  leaves from, which is not a station but the point London is measured from, and a quarter to nine.
