**Hook of the day (one line):** Verne wrote a novel about going round the world and then skipped Europe — everything the book says about the next three days is seven lines in a notebook, and it takes twenty seconds to read. (F-49, F-50)

**Narrative arc (three acts):**
- **Act I — Everything he wrote, and then the sea** (segs 1–3, ≈ 355 s). The notebook is read out and runs out; we go to Dover, where the train ran onto the pier and you stepped from carriage to deck; the Channel at night; and at Calais the answer to *why this route existed at all* — a mail train for India. **The turn:** the route is not a detour, it is the fastest thing in the world, and it is two years old.
- **Act II — The city he was driven across** (seg 4, ≈ 210 s). Eighty minutes of Paris through the window of a cab, in the rain. He names two places he wishes he could see; neither is on the road; one had just become a mass grave and the other no longer exists. And the thing he does not name: the palace in the middle of the city, still a burnt shell.
- **Act III — The hole through the mountain, and the door out of Europe** (segs 5–9, ≈ 560 s). The tunnel that made the route possible, opened thirteen months before Fogg's train; the man who invented the machine that dug it and died two months before it opened; forty-five minutes in Turin one kilometre from the world's oldest Egyptian museum, on the way to Egypt; and the end of the Appian Way, where Britain's mail for India got on a ship. **Ends on departure**, 5 pm, Saturday 5 October 1872, so Day 3 opens at sea.

**Guide voice:** the modern guide (personas/guide.md), first-person plural, unhurried. No dialogue persona this chapter (see Alternatives 1 — a Passepartout dialogue is the strongest optional addition and I have costed it). Period voices come from two dead men who are free to quote: Verne, and Dickens crossing the same water in 1863 (F-89…F-92).

**Newcomer rule applied (style guide, Audience Report #1):** the spine retells. Every meta-beat — Towle's *fiacre* mistranslation (F-54), the "Wednesday, not Friday" slip (F-61), the postcard's caption (F-108), the illustrator skipping Europe (F-109) — is told **only after** the plain version, and three of the four live in optional "go deeper" pins, not in the narration. Nobody needs to have read the novel to follow this chapter; nobody needs to know what a *fiacre* is before we show them one.

## Density budget — the hard constraint

Day 1's three worst scenes were over-packed in English before translation ever exposed them. This chapter is written to a
**ceiling of 80 % narration fill per segment, and the ceiling is never reached** — the highest segment here is 75 % (the
tunnel, the one genuinely information-dense beat) and the chapter average is **65 %**.

**Fill** = the share of scene seconds in which the guide is speaking. **Word ceiling** = `duration × fill × 2.4 words/s`
(R3 allows 2.5; I budget 2.4 so the Narrator has room). Anything a caption, pin or card already shows on screen is **not**
also read aloud (R3) — that is how the tunnel diagram carries its numbers and the notebook card carries its seven lines.

## Detailed specs the Scene Developer will need

### Quiz C (scene 03) — "Before any of this existed, how long did a letter from London take to reach India?"
Soft, curiosity-shaped, skippable; feedback teaches on every option (style guide; positioning "never school"). R5: none of this wording appears in the preceding two minutes.
- (a) "About three weeks" — wrong. Feedback: "Not until steamships and the canal. Three weeks is roughly what it took *after* everything in this chapter was built." (F-62, F-64)
- (b) "About six weeks" — wrong, and the interesting wrong answer. Feedback: "That was the *new* way. In the 1830s Thomas Waghorn pieced together a route through Egypt — ship, river, camels and carriages across the desert, ship again — and got it to about six weeks." (F-62)
- (c) "About three months" — **correct**. Feedback: "Round the Cape of Good Hope, over eleven thousand miles. Three months each way — so a question and its answer took half a year." (F-62)
Guide tail: "That is why a train to the heel of Italy counts as a short cut." Visual: **G-206**.

### Quiz D (scene 07) — "It is Thursday night and the train is climbing. Two years before Fogg, how did this train get to the other side of the Alps?"
- (a) "Everyone changed to a coach in summer, and a sledge in winter" — wrong *now*, right *earlier*. Feedback: "True until 1868 — and it is why crossing the Alps was a story people told for the rest of their lives." (F-73)
- (b) "A railway laid along the mountain road over the pass, gripping a third rail in the middle" — **correct**. Feedback: "Seventy-seven kilometres over the top, summit 2,283 m, gradients of one in eleven, about five hours — and the mail for India went over it." (F-73, plate M-240)
- (c) "The carriages were carried through a tunnel on flat wagons" — wrong. Feedback: "There was no tunnel yet. That is what the next ten minutes are about." (F-69)
Guide tail: "In 1867 you crossed these mountains in the open air. In 1872 you slept through them." (F-73)
