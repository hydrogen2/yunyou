## B. Why the route existed: the Overland Route and the Indian Mail

| id | fact | confidence | sources | notes |
|----|------|-----------|---------|-------|
| F-62 | Before the overland route, mail and passengers for India went by sea round the **Cape of Good Hope** — over 11,000 miles, and mail steamers took **about three months**. Lieut. **Thomas Fletcher Waghorn** (1800–50) pioneered a route via Egypt in the 1830s: steamer to Alexandria, river and canal to Cairo, horses and carriages across the desert to Suez, steamer down the Red Sea. He claimed it cut the journey to about **6,000 miles and 35–45 days**, and between 1835 and 1837 he set up seven rest-houses across the desert. | high | [5][6] | This is the "why on earth go by land?" answer, in one contrast: three months vs six weeks. The word "overland" survives from the desert stage even after there was no desert stage left. |
| F-63 | **The Indian Mail** — in Italian, **la Valigia delle Indie** ("the India suitcase"); in Verne's French, **la malle des Indes** — was the through service London → Brindisi by train, then P&O steamer to Alexandria/Port Said and on to Bombay. The first P&O sailing from Brindisi was on **25 October 1870**; the service ran from Brindisi until **1914**. | high | [7][8] | Note the date: **two years before Fogg**. Verne is writing about a service that is brand new. |
| F-64 | The point of Brindisi was time. The mail convoy left **London on Friday at 21:00 and reached Brindisi on Sunday at 18:00 — about 45 hours** — and reached Bombay in **22 days, against 25 days for a sailing from Marseille**. P&O steamers left Brindisi for Egypt on Sundays. | med | [7][9] | The 45 h figure is from the Italian Wikipedia account of the service and may describe the later, faster Peninsular Express rather than October 1872 exactly. Use as "about two days" and flag. Compare F-51: Fogg took ~66 h and was *slower than the post*. |
| F-65 | Brindisi could only be the port because the railway had already got there: **Brindisi station opened in 1865** as part of the Adriatic line, ending the rail isolation of the Italian "heel" and connecting Puglia to Ancona and the north. | high | [10][11] | Cause and effect worth stating plainly: rails to the heel (1865) + Suez Canal (1869) + Fréjus tunnel (1871) = Brindisi. |
| F-66 | P&O's mail business began with an Admiralty contract in **1837** (Iberia) and a contract to carry mail to **Alexandria in 1840**; Southampton was its home port. Brindisi was added in the 1870s. After the **Suez Canal opened in November 1869** the steamers could run straight through to India, and the "overland" part of the Overland Route shrank to a train ride across Europe. | med | [12][8] | The canal is Day 1's F-33 enabler; here it is what makes Brindisi worth having. |
| F-67 | The traffic built a hotel: an establishment "inaugurated in 1870" at Brindisi for Indian Mail passengers survives today as the **Hotel International**. | med | [8] | Single source (a P&O enthusiast site). Present-day traveller action, so Media should verify the hotel still trades before we put it on a card. |
| F-68 | The Brindisi era ended in **1914**, when P&O moved back to Marseille — wartime exposure of the Adriatic, and Italy's unwillingness to upgrade the port. Forty-four years of being the door to India, then nothing. | med | [7][8] | A quiet, unhurried ending beat for the chapter if we want one. |

## C. The keystone: the Fréjus (Mont Cenis) tunnel

| id | fact | confidence | sources | notes |
|----|------|-----------|---------|-------|
| F-69 | The **Fréjus Rail Tunnel** — universally called the **Mont Cenis Tunnel** at the time, and by Verne — links **Modane** (France) and **Bardonecchia** (Italy). Digging began **August 1857** on the Bardonecchia side and **December 1857** at Modane; the two headings met on **26 December 1870**; it opened to traffic on **17 September 1871**. It was about **12.8 km** long at opening (13.7 km today) at a mean altitude of **1,123 m**, running under the Col du Fréjus (2,542 m). | high | [13][14][15] | **Thirteen months before Fogg's train.** This completes the Day 1 set: Suez 17 Nov 1869, Promontory 10 May 1869, Fréjus 17 Sept 1871. The inaugural train, 22 carriages, left Bardonecchia at 10:30 and reached Modane at 11:00. |
| F-70 | It was the third-longest thing humans had ever dug and then some: at completion it was **twice the length of any tunnel previously constructed**, and it remained the **longest tunnel in the world until the Gotthard opened in 1882**. | high | [13][14] | Say it plainly rather than as a superlative pile-up: "nobody had ever made a hole in the world this long." |
| F-71 | The tunnel was only possible because of a machine. **Germain Sommeiller** (1815–71), a Savoyard engineer who chose Italian citizenship when Savoy went to France in 1860, patented a **compressed-air rock drill** (building on Giovanni Battista Piatti's invention) and ran the works with **Sebastiano Grandis** and **Severino Grattoni**. Ten drills were mounted in a frame and fed with air at **7 atmospheres** by water-powered compressors; each blast needed up to **eighty holes**, taking **six to eight hours** to drill. Hand-drilling would reportedly have taken about **71 years**. | high | [13][15] | The compressed air also ventilated the workings — the drills' exhaust was the fresh air. That is the detail people remember. |
| F-72 | **Sommeiller died on 11 July 1871**, at Saint-Jeoire where he was born — two months before the tunnel he had spent fourteen years digging was opened. Bardonecchia has a Via Germano Sommeiller. | high | [15][16] | The chapter's one genuinely moving fact, and it needs no decoration. |
| F-73 | **What travellers did before it.** From **15 June 1868 to 15 October 1871**, the **Mont Cenis Pass Railway** carried the Indian Mail and its passengers *over* the mountain: **77 km**, largely along the road, summit **2,283 m**, gradients up to **1 in 11 (9 %)**, using John Barraclough Fell's centre-rail system, about **5 hours 15 minutes** Susa → St Michel. Before *that*, rail passengers crossed the Alps by **horse-drawn stagecoach in summer and sledge in winter**; the Fell line saved about six hours and cost 20 francs less in first class. | high | [17] | The contrast is the point: in 1867 you crossed the Alps in the open air on a sledge; in 1872 you slept through them. The Fell company had built for a longer life and lost money when the tunnel opened early. |
| F-74 | The French approach was finished to match: the last section of the **Culoz–Modane line** (the Maurienne valley line), Saint-Michel to Modane and the tunnel mouth, opened on **16 October 1871** — the day after the Fell railway shut. Fogg's Paris → Turin run therefore went Paris (Gare de Lyon) → Dijon/Mâcon → Culoz → Chambéry → Saint-Jean-de-Maurienne → Modane → tunnel → Bardonecchia → Turin. | med | [18][17] | The station list is the standard route of the line; I have not seen an October 1872 timetable, so present it as "the line ran through", not "his train stopped at". |
| F-75 | **Today the same tunnel is still the way through, and it has just come back.** A rockfall of about **10,000 m³** in the Maurienne valley on **27 August 2023** closed the line for **nineteen months**; it reopened on **31 March 2025** (repairs ≈ €13 m), and direct **TGV INOUI** Paris–Chambéry–Turin–Milan services resumed the same day, with Trenitalia Frecciarossa following on 1 April 2025. | high | [19][20] | Genuinely actionable, and a good "the Alps still get a vote" beat. Media should re-check the service is running at publication. |

## D. The places, then and now

| id | fact | confidence | sources | notes |
|----|------|-----------|---------|-------|
| F-76 | **Dover.** The **Admiralty Pier** was begun in **1847**; the **South Eastern Railway ran its rails out onto the still-unfinished pier in 1861** so that boat trains could set passengers down beside the packet; by 1872 the pier was about **2,100 ft (640 m)** long and its first phase was complete (1871). Fogg's boat train from Charing Cross would have run out onto it. | med | [21][22] | The Dover Historian site was 403 to fetch; figures are from search snippets plus the Wikipedia Admiralty Pier article. Flagged in Gaps. |
| F-77 | **The white cliffs** are chalk laid down in the **Late Cretaceous, roughly 100–66 million years ago**, streaked with black flint; they reach about **110 m (350 ft)** and run about **13 km (8 miles)** either side of Dover. At the Strait's narrowest the French coast is about **32 km (20 miles)** away and visible on a clear day. The National Trust's **Gateway visitor centre** opened in 1999. | high | [23] | The cliffs are the one thing on this route Fogg could not have avoided seeing — and Verne does not mention them. Nice inversion. |
| F-78 | **Dover–Calais today:** about **90 minutes** by ferry, with DFDS, P&O Ferries and Irish Ferries running as often as every 30 minutes at peak and up to ~30 sailings a day. For comparison, **Eurostar** runs London St Pancras → Paris Gare du Nord in about **2 h 16 min**, up to 18 times a day — roughly a fifth of Fogg's 10½ hours. | med | [24][25] | Ferry and Eurostar times are commercial-site figures; treat as "about". Do not put prices on a card — they move. |
| F-79 | **Calais.** Dickens's night packet did not land at the old jetty but "went steaming up the harbour to the **Railway Station Quay**" — the Gare Maritime, where the boat handed passengers straight to the Paris train. From Calais the Chemin de fer du Nord ran to Paris; the main line reached Amiens and Lille in **1846**, Boulogne (via Amiens) in **1848**, and Calais from Lille on **1 September 1848**. | high | [26][27] | Two Calais routes existed by 1872 (via Lille/Arras and via Boulogne/Amiens); Dickens's train in 1863 was called for "Hazebroucke, Lille, Douai, Bruxelles, Arras, Amiens, and Paris". Which one Fogg's boat train used is unverified — see Gaps. |
| F-80 | **The Amiens coincidence.** The Calais–Paris main line runs through **Amiens** — and **Jules Verne settled in Amiens in 1871** and lived there until his death in 1905, writing *Le Tour du monde en quatre-vingts jours* there. Fogg's night train passed through the town where the man imagining it was asleep. | high | [28][29] | Strong, cheap, true. The Maison de Jules Verne museum is the house he occupied **1882–1900**, so do not say "the house he wrote the novel in". |
| F-81 | **Gare du Nord.** The first station opened 14 June 1846; the present building, by **Jacques Ignace Hittorff**, was built 1861–64 and came into use in 1864, its façade carrying allegorical statues of the cities it served — Paris at the top, eight international cities around her. It is today **Europe's busiest railway station** and the London Eurostar terminus. | high (1864/Hittorff) / med (busiest, figures) | [30] | Passepartout arrived under a façade that was eight years old — Verne's Europe is new, exactly as Day 1's London was. Do not quote the raw annual passenger number; sources disagree wildly. |
| F-82 | **Gare de Lyon.** Opened 12 August 1849; a second building of 1855 had five tracks under a hall 220 m × 42 m — and **that station was partly destroyed by fire during the Paris Commune in 1871** and rebuilt. Passepartout's Italy train left from a station that had burned sixteen months earlier. The famous clock tower and Le Train Bleu belong to the **1900–01** rebuilding, not to 1872. | high | [31] | Guard against the obvious mistake: nobody in 1872 saw the Gare de Lyon we photograph today. |
| F-83 | **Paris in October 1872 was a city with its centre burnt out.** The **Tuileries Palace** was set alight by Communards on 23 May 1871 and burned for two days, leaving only walls; the ruins stood behind a palisade for eleven years and were only demolished between February and 30 September **1883**. The Hôtel de Ville burned in the same week. Verne, a Frenchman writing in 1872, says nothing whatever about any of this. | high | [32][33] | The single strongest "what he did not see" beat available. Handle with the house tone — unhurried, no melodrama. |
| F-84 | **Père-Lachaise**, which Passepartout wished he could see again: founded **1804**, 44 hectares, more than a million burials, in the 20th arrondissement. Against its south-east wall — the **Mur des Fédérés** — about **147 Communards** were shot and buried in a common grave during the Bloody Week of **May 1871**, seventeen months before Passepartout was driven past Paris in the rain. It is still a working cemetery; Métro Père Lachaise (lines 2, 3) and Philippe Auguste (line 2). | high | [34] | Passepartout is nostalgic for a place that had just become a mass grave. Say it once, quietly, and move on. |
| F-85 | **The circus in the Champs-Élysées** was real and famous: built **1841** to **Hittorff's** design at the Rond-Point, a 16-sided building 42 m across with a **13.5 m ring**, 20 m high. Called Cirque National, then **Cirque de l'Impératrice** from 1853 (for Empress Eugénie) until the Empire fell in 1870, then **Cirque d'Été**. It was demolished around **1900**. Its name survives in the **rue du Cirque**, Paris 8e. | high | [35][36] | Note the timing: by October 1872 the Empress's circus had just lost its Empress. Also ties to Day 1's F-31/Passepartout's circus-rider past ("vault like Leotard... dance on a rope like Blondin") — he is homesick for his own trade. |
| F-86 | **Turin** had been the **first capital of united Italy, 1861–65**, and had lost the title seven years before Fogg passed through. **Torino Porta Nuova**, begun 1861 under Alessandro Mazzucchetti, opened to the public in **December 1864** and was finished in **1868** — so Fogg's 45-minute wait was in a four-year-old station. It is today **Italy's third busiest station** (after Roma Termini and Milano Centrale), about 350 trains and ~192,000 journeys a day. | high (dates) / med (traffic figures) | [37][38] | The preserved first-class waiting room, the **Sala Gonin**, is frescoed with the Earth, Water, Fire and the four continents — a waiting room decorated with the whole world, for a man crossing it. Media should check public access. |
| F-87 | **Turin's joke on Fogg.** The **Museo Egizio**, founded in **1824**, is the world's oldest museum devoted entirely to ancient Egypt and holds the second most important Egyptological collection anywhere after Cairo — over 37,000 items on display, 1,036,689 visitors in 2024. It stands at Via Accademia delle Scienze 6, about **1 km from Porta Nuova**, and has been in that building since the 1830s. Fogg waited 45 minutes a walk away from it, on his way to Egypt. | high | [39] | The best stop-and-look candidate in the chapter after Paris. Verifiable, walkable, and it lands the joke without a word of comment. |
| F-88 | **Brindisi** is the end of the **Appian Way**. Two Roman columns marked the terminus; **one still stands, 18.74 m (61.5 ft)** high, at the top of the harbour steps — the other collapsed in **1582** and its remains went to Lecce. **Virgil died at Brindisi in 19 BC**. Population 87,141 (end-2017); the airport handled 2.32 m passengers in 2017. The port still runs ferries to **Greece (Igoumenitsa, Corfu, Patras) and Albania (Vlorë)**, so it is still where you leave Europe by sea — just eastward, not southward. | high (column, Virgil) / med (figures) | [40][41] | The road that carried Rome's mail east ends at the quay where Britain's mail for India began. That is the chapter's closing image and it is a fact, not a flourish. |

## E. Sensory, human, and the language layer

| id | fact | confidence | sources | notes |
|----|------|-----------|---------|-------|
| F-89 | **A primary account of exactly this crossing, in the dark, by a very famous witness.** Charles Dickens, "The Calais Night Mail", *All the Year Round*, **2 May 1863**, collected in *The Uncommercial Traveller* — public domain. He waits on the deck at Dover: "A screech, a bell, and two red eyes come gliding down the Admiralty Pier"; the mail vans open and "stooping figures with sacks upon their backs begin to be beheld among the piles"; the passengers are "a few shadowy Frenchmen, with hatboxes shaped like the stoppers of gigantic case-bottles; a few shadowy Germans in immense fur coats and boots; a few shadowy Englishmen prepared for the worst and pretending not to expect it." | high | [42][43] | Use sparingly and attributed. The last clause is the funniest sentence available to this chapter and it is free to use. |
| F-90 | Dickens on the crossing itself: the boat "rumble, hum, scream, roar, and establish an immense family washing-day at each paddle-box"; the funnel "gives a complaining roar"; the passengers "lie about in melancholy bundles, as if they were sorted out for the laundress"; and one man clutches an upright umbrella all the way over — "Is there any analogy, in certain constitutions, between keeping an umbrella up, and keeping the spirits up?" | high | [42] | Sound-design brief in a paragraph: paddle wash, funnel, wind, bell, hawser. |
| F-91 | Dickens's landfall at Calais: they steam up to the Railway Station Quay while "the bells of Calais striking One" sound; hotel touts call "Hôtel Dessin! ... The Royal Hotel, Sir, Angaishe ouse!" and "You going to Parry, Sir? Your baggage, registair froo, Sir?"; customs officers are "in green and grey"; and the station buffet offers "savoury roast fowls, hot coffee, cognac, and Bordeaux". | high | [42] | The **language shift happens in the mouths of the touts**, not in a lesson: English spoken as French heard. This is the natural home of the chapter's language layer. |
| F-92 | Dickens's Calais–Paris compartment holds "a compatriot in an obsolete cravat, who thinks it a quite unaccountable thing that they don't keep 'London time' on a French railway", and a young priest with a bird in a cage. The guard clips tickets by **climbing along the outside of the train at full speed**, holding the open window by his elbows — carriages of this period were compartments without corridors. | high | [42] | Pays off Day 1's running thread of Passepartout's slow watch (world-bible: the watch is set to London and stays there) — here is a real Englishman doing the same thing, nine years earlier. |
| F-93 | The Channel crossing was bad enough that a rich man tried to engineer it away. **Sir Henry Bessemer**, a chronic sufferer, patented in **December 1869** a ship whose passenger saloon hung on gimbals and was kept level by hydraulics. The **SS Bessemer** launched 24 September 1874; on her one public voyage, **8 May 1875**, the swinging saloon was locked and she rammed and demolished part of Calais pier. The company folded in 1876; she was scrapped in 1879. | high | [44] | Three years after Fogg, so it is context, not scene. Use as the "how bad was it, really?" answer. |
| F-94 | **French words a traveller actually meets on this route**, all of them from the sources above rather than a phrasebook: *un fiacre* — a horse-cab (Verne's word for Passepartout's Paris crossing); *la gare* — the station; *le quai* — platform, and also quay; *la douane* — customs; *la malle des Indes* — the Indian Mail; *le buffet* — the station restaurant; *la Manche* — the Channel (literally "the sleeve"). | high | [2][42][45] | Deliberately small. Six words, each attached to something on screen. |
| F-95 | **Italian words for the second half:** *il traforo* — the tunnel; *la stazione marittima* — the harbour station, where the train meets the ship, and still the name of the quay at Brindisi; *il binario* — the platform/track; *la valigia* — the suitcase, as in *la Valigia delle Indie*; *le colonne romane* — the Roman columns at Brindisi. | high | [7][40][45] | *Valigia* is the good one: the whole service was named after a piece of luggage, and Fogg is travelling with one carpet-bag. |

## Suggested angles for the writer

- **The spine, stated as a fact and then walked.** F-49 is the whole chapter in one line: Verne skips Europe. Open by reading Fogg's notebook (F-50) end to end — it takes twenty seconds — and say plainly that this is everything the novel contains about the next three days. Then spend eighteen minutes on what is in the gap. The emotional payoff the brief asks for is structural, not rhetorical: we do not need to *argue* that Passepartout missed things, we just go and look at them.
- **"It would do his eyes good."** (F-56) Plant this on the boat train before Dover. Pay it off at the Gare du Nord with F-53 and F-54: he did get to Paris, and he got eighty minutes of it, through a cab window, in the rain. Then walk the traveller the same 4 km (F-98 → F-99) in the dry, slowly.
- **The Paris beat answers the brief's open question.** Yes, stop in Paris — but stop for the *absence*. The two things he names (F-55) are a cemetery he can't reach and a circus that no longer exists (F-84, F-85). One is a mass grave seventeen months old; the other is a street name. That is a better Paris scene than the Eiffel Tower, and it is entirely on-theme for `timeline`.
- **The city he could not have missed, and Verne never mentions.** F-83: Paris in October 1872 still had the Tuileries standing as a burnt shell, behind a fence, for another eleven years. Passepartout crossed a wounded city and complained about a circus. Say it once. No moralising.
- **Three dates, one shrunken world — now complete.** Day 1 lit Suez (17 Nov 1869) and Promontory (10 May 1869). F-69 lights the third: 17 September 1871, thirteen months before the wager. The map beat is already built; this chapter closes it. And F-73 is the contrast that gives it weight — in 1867 you crossed these mountains on a sledge.
- **Sommeiller.** (F-71, F-72) Fourteen years, a machine he had to invent first, and he died two months before the opening. This is the one place in the chapter where the tone should slow right down and then stop. No music sting.
- **The tunnel is still the argument.** F-75: closed by a rockfall in 2023, reopened 31 March 2025. The Alps have not finished having opinions. Also the most useful present-day fact in the sheet — a traveller can book that train.
- **Turin is a 45-minute joke.** (F-86, F-87) Fogg waits 45 minutes in Porta Nuova. One kilometre away is the oldest museum of ancient Egypt in the world, and he is on his way to Egypt. Stop-and-look candidate #1 (D6-compliant: there is something specific to find). The Sala Gonin's four continents in the first-class waiting room is a second, if Media can get access.
- **Dickens is our second guide for the Channel.** (F-89 to F-92) Public domain, primary, funny, and it solves the density budget: let his sentences carry the crossing and keep our own narration under it. "A few shadowy Englishmen prepared for the worst and pretending not to expect it" is the best line available to this chapter and it is not ours.
- **The language layer belongs to the touts.** (F-91, F-94, F-95) Don't teach French; let the traveller hear "Angaishe ouse!" and "registair froo" and understand that the border is a sound. Six French words, five Italian, each pinned to something on screen.
- **The watch thread.** Dickens's compatriot who wants London time on a French railway (F-92) is a free callback to Passepartout's slow watch from Day 1, and it sets up Fix's line at Suez in Day 3. Cheap, warm, and it makes the chapters feel like one journey.
- **Closing image.** The Appian Way ends at Brindisi (F-88); the Indian Mail began there (F-63, F-107); the two are 150 m apart. Rome's road east, Britain's road east, same quay. End on the *Mongolia* leaving (the brief's recommendation) with the column in frame.
- **Souvenir candidates.** (a) Fogg's notebook page (F-50) as a printable card, with a blank column for the traveller's own times; (b) the three enabler dates as a single card — 1869, 1869, 1871; (c) a Brindisi card: the column, the Stazione Marittima, and the ferry that still leaves for Greece.
- **Do not** stage the Channel crossing as a scene at Dover Priory. Fogg's train ran out onto the Admiralty Pier (F-76) and he stepped from carriage to deck. The modern equivalent is the ferry terminal, not the station.

## Gaps / things I could not verify

- **No October 1872 Bradshaw was consulted** (the same gap as Day 1). Which actual boat train Fogg would have caught, which Calais–Paris line it used (via Lille/Arras or via Boulogne/Amiens — F-79), and whether his Paris–Turin train really called at the stations in F-74, are all unverified. Present the route as "the line ran", never "his train stopped at".
- **The 45-hour London→Brindisi mail schedule (F-64)** comes from the Italian Wikipedia account and may describe the later, faster Peninsular Express rather than the service running in October 1872. The comparison with Fogg's ~66 hours (F-51) is therefore suggestive, not proven. If the writer wants to use "Fogg was slower than the post", hedge it.
- **The real *Mongolia* (F-59):** P&O Heritage's own ship fact sheet returned HTTP 403. Tonnage figures differ across sources (2,999 GRT vs Verne's 2,800). I could not confirm she was on the Brindisi–Bombay run specifically in October 1872, nor her master's name, nor passenger capacity.
- **Dover's Admiralty Pier (F-76):** doverhistorian.com blocked the fetch; the 1861 SER extension onto the pier and the 2,100 ft length rest on search snippets. Worth one retry before the Fact-Checker sees it.
- **Whether the Admiralty Pier station as such existed in 1872** (as opposed to rails on the pier) I could not settle; Dover Marine/Western Docks is a 20th-century station and must not be used for 1872.
- **The Italian route Turin → Brindisi in 1872** — I have the Adriatic line and the 1865 arrival at Brindisi, but not the intermediate stations or the running time for that year. The it.wikipedia list (Turin, Piacenza, Bologna, Ancona, Pescara, Foggia, Brindisi) is for the later Peninsular Express; do not present it as Fogg's stops.
- **Anachronism warning:** the wagons-lits sleeping cars and dining car associated with the Valigia delle Indie belong to the 1890s, not 1872. CIWL was not founded until 1872 and did not run this train then. Fogg and Passepartout sat up all night in compartments (F-92).
- **Weather** on the Channel on the night of 2–3 October 1872 — unverified. Verne says driving rain in Paris the next morning (F-53); Day 1's F-13 gives rain in London. Do not invent a storm.
- **Turin's Sala Gonin** — I could not confirm whether it is open to the public, or on what terms. Media must check before we send anyone.
- **Brindisi's Hotel International (F-67)** — single enthusiast source; confirm it still trades before it goes on a card.
- **Gare du Nord passenger numbers** — sources give wildly different annual figures (one infobox reads 257 million, which cannot be right for a single station). Use "Europe's busiest" without a number, or find an SNCF/Ile-de-France Mobilités figure.
- **Verne's Amiens address in 1872 (F-80)** — he settled in Amiens in 1871, but the museum house is the one he took in 1882. Which house he was in while writing *Le Tour du monde* I did not establish.
- I did **not** research media assets, licences or YouTube ids — that is the Content Preparer's job. No coordinates here have been checked against Street View or open imagery coverage.

## Digest
Read Verne's chapters III–VIII in both English and French, found that the novel simply skips the whole of Europe — which turns the brief's spine into a documented fact rather than an interpretation — and built 48 facts (F-48…F-107) around the three things that fill the gap: why the Brindisi route existed, the Fréjus tunnel that made it fast, and the places Passepartout named and never saw.
Weakest parts: no 1872 timetable, so every claim about *which* train and *which* line is hedged; the 45-hour mail schedule and the real *Mongolia*'s details rest on blocked or enthusiast sources; and several present-day operational facts (ferry times, the Sala Gonin, the Brindisi hotel) need re-checking near publication.
With more time I would find a scanned October 1872 Bradshaw's Continental and an 1871–72 Indian Mail timetable, retry the three blocked sources (P&O Heritage, Dover Historian, Britannica), and look for a first-hand traveller's account of a passage *through* the new tunnel in 1871–73 to sit beside Dickens on the Channel.
